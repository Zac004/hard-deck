Terrain models

The three surfaces every height hangs from

A height is a distance above some surface. Most large height errors come from two sources quoting heights above two different surfaces. Three surfaces matter.

SurfaceWhat it isWho uses it
WGS84 ellipsoidA perfectly smooth, slightly flattened ball chosen to fit the Earth. Pure geometry.GNSS satellites and receivers, inertial and GNSS navigation computers internally, radar terrain processing.
Geoid: EGM96, EGM2008The shape the sea would take at rest, continued under the land. It follows gravity, so it has bumps and hollows.Charts, DTED, airfield elevations, barometric altimetry, anything called “above mean sea level”.
GroundThe terrain itself, or in a surface model the top of whatever covers it.You.

The gap between the first two is the geoid undulation, N. Sea-level height H equals ellipsoid height h minus N. Over India N is negative everywhere, from about −24 m at Leh to about −97 m at Thiruvananthapuram, because the subcontinent lies on the edge of the Indian Ocean geoid low, the deepest hollow in the geoid anywhere on Earth.

EGM96 and EGM2008 are two models of the same geoid. EGM2008 was built with far more gravity data and is the better physical model. EGM96 is the one aviation data, DTED, SRTM and AW3D30 are tied to. Over most of India they differ by 0.2 to 1 m; in the high Himalaya by more.

How HARD DECK arrives at its number
  1. One common footing. Each source is converted to height above the ellipsoid using the geoid it was built on. After that, differences between sources are differences in the terrain, with the reference removed.
  2. Independent sources only. Models made from the same satellite data share the same errors, so they count once. The families are TanDEM-X radar (Copernicus GLO-30 and GLO-90, FABDEM, TanDEM-X 90), Shuttle radar (NASADEM, SRTM, the ASF PALSAR DEM), ALOS optical (AW3D30), and a DTED file you load. The most detailed member present speaks for its family.
  3. The median. The estimate is the middle value of those families: with three, one wrong source cannot move it. With two it is their mean, with one it is that source.
  4. Files under test. A raster of your own that the app does not recognise is compared against the estimate and never feeds it, unless it is the only source. A DTED file counts as its own family, except when it matches the Shuttle radar data at the point to within about 3 m: then it is that data, and shares its vote.
  5. The ± figure. It is the larger of two things: half the gap between the independent sources, and a rule of thumb for 30 m models on the slope measured at the point (about 2 m on flat ground, 4 m on gentle slopes, 8 m in hills, 15 m on steep ground, 25 m on very steep ground). It is an honest indication, and it is not a guarantee.
  6. Between posts. Heights between grid posts come from the four posts around the point, weighted by distance.
  7. The geoids. EGM96 is held at 15 arc-minutes and EGM2008 at 2.5 arc-minutes over South Asia (4° to 40°N, 58° to 100°E). Checked against the full EGM96 model at 3,000 random points the built-in values differ from it by about 1 cm (RMS) and by 12 cm at worst. Elsewhere in the world EGM2008 is held at 15 arc-minutes: usually within 10 cm, up to about 0.75 m in the highest mountains, and the app says so when it applies.

DTED, and how to check a DTED file

DTED is a matrix of elevation posts on a latitude and longitude grid, one file for each 1° cell. Each post is a whole number of metres above mean sea level (EGM96), on WGS84 horizontally. The standard is MIL-PRF-89020B.

LevelPost spacingPosts per cellHorizontal objectiveVertical objective
Level 030 arc-seconds, about 900 m121 × 121none of its ownnone of its own
Level 13 arc-seconds, about 90 m1201 × 120150 m, 90% circular error30 m, 90% linear error
Level 21 arc-second, about 30 m3601 × 360123 m, 90% circular error18 m, 90% linear error

Point-to-point (relative) vertical accuracy is 20 m for Level 1, and for Level 2 it is 12 m on slopes up to 20% and 15 m on steeper ground. Beyond 50° of latitude the posts are spaced wider in longitude. Levels 3, 4 and 5 (roughly 10 m, 3 m and 1 m) were proposed and never standardised; finer military data is issued in other formats.

Where the heights come from. Public Level 1 and Level 2 files were built from the SRTM radar flight of February 2000, with water flattened and gaps edited, so they include trees and buildings. Other DTED was compiled from stereo imagery and maps and can represent the bare ground. The header does not always say which.

To check a file here

  1. Open Data, then “Your files”, and add the .dt1 or .dt2 file. HARD DECK reads the header (corner, post count, level, vertical reference code, stated accuracy, security marking) and lists it.
  2. Go to Point. A “DTED cross-check” panel appears whenever the point lies inside the file. It gives the DTED height, the public models’ estimate, the difference, and whether that difference is inside the level’s vertical objective.
  3. Switch on “SRTM 1 arc-second” under Sources. If your DTED was built from SRTM the two agree to within a metre or two.
  4. Go to Area, study the cell or part of it, choose “Difference, A minus B” with DTED as A and Copernicus or SRTM as B. Read the mean offset, the spread and the “90% within” figure against the objective in the table above.

Posts are stored as sign-and-magnitude numbers with −32767 marking a void. Some producers wrote negative heights in two’s complement instead; HARD DECK detects that and reads them correctly, and tells you it did.

A DTED file stays on this device. If its header carries a security code other than U, handle it under your own rules and consider EMCON on the Data page, which stops all lookups and map requests.

Worked example: the elevation of a waypoint in the aircraft

An inertial and GNSS unit of the TOTEM 3000 class computes position on the WGS84 ellipsoid, because that is what GNSS provides, and blends in barometric height to keep the vertical channel stable. Elevations on charts and in terrain files are sea-level heights. Somewhere a geoid correction bridges the two.

  • Cause. A waypoint elevation is taken from a source that quotes ellipsoid height, such as a raw GNSS survey or the ASF PALSAR DEM, and typed into a field that expects sea-level elevation.
  • Effect on height. At Gwalior the waypoint ends up 58 m (190 ft) too low, at Thiruvananthapuram 97 m (318 ft), at Leh 24 m (79 ft).
  • Effect on position. Whenever a line of sight is intersected with that elevation, the height error becomes a ground error of height error divided by the tangent of the look-down angle. A 58 m error is about 330 m on the ground at 10° and about 100 m at 30°. Steeper geometry forgives more.
  • What to do. Read the point here, take the “MSL EGM96” value where a chart-style elevation is expected, and carry the ± figure as your uncertainty. Which reference a particular field in a particular aircraft expects is defined in that aircraft’s documentation, so confirm it there.

Barometric altitude is a separate matter. An altimeter set to QNH shows true altitude only in a standard atmosphere. In air warmer than standard the aircraft is higher than indicated, and in colder air lower, by roughly 4% of the height above the station for every 10 °C. That is weather, and no terrain or geoid model corrects it.

Desert, mountains, and making DTED from radar

Rajasthan desert. Open, dry, gently sloping ground with almost no vegetation is the easy case. Expect the 30 m models to agree within 1 to 3 m, with Copernicus the most reliable. The exception is active dunes, where the surface has moved since the data was collected: trust interdune flats, rock and built-up ground more than dune crests.

Himalaya. Expect 5 to 15 m of disagreement on slopes and more on cliffs and ridgelines. Compare Copernicus with AW3D30, which do not share a sensor: where they agree the value is sound, and where they differ by more than about 15 m the point is on steep or shadowed ground and neither deserves more trust than that. Summits read low in every 30 m model. EGM96 and EGM2008 also differ most here.

Building DTED from radar. Radar interferometry gives ellipsoid heights. DTED is sea-level height on EGM96. Subtract the EGM96 undulation from every cell before calling the product DTED, and when checking against Copernicus remember that it sits on EGM2008. An Area study shows how much N changes across your tile: if that range is larger than your accuracy target, a single correction for the whole tile is not good enough. Note also that the widely shared “ALOS PALSAR 12.5 m DEM” from ASF is SRTM resampled to a finer grid, with ellipsoid heights, and contains no PALSAR height measurement.

Reading the Area statistics
  • Mean offset is a constant shift between two models. Tens of metres means a reference mix-up. A few metres over forest means surface against bare earth.
  • Spread (σ) is the random part left once the constant shift is removed. It grows with slope and roughness.
  • RMSE combines both into one figure, the usual headline accuracy number.
  • 90% within is the limit that nine cells in ten stay inside. It is directly comparable with the 90% linear error in the DTED specification.
  • Slope is worked out on the sampling grid, so on a coarse grid over a large area it reads gentler than the ground really is.
On a phone, offline, and under EMCON
  • Install it. On an iPhone open the site in Safari, tap Share, then “Add to Home Screen”. On Android open it in Chrome and choose “Install app” or “Add to Home screen”. It then opens full screen like any other app.
  • Offline. After one visit the app and the South Asia geoids are kept on the device. Without a connection it still converts between references and reads any DTED, GeoTIFF or .hgt files you load. Online sources and the satellite map need a connection.
  • EMCON. The switch on the Data page, or a tap on the status light at the top, stops all elevation lookups, map pictures and location requests, and stops the app remembering the last point. Use it with your own tiles for coordinates that should not leave the device. The app’s own files may still be fetched from the address it is hosted at.
  • Your location. The arrow button beside the coordinate box fills in where the device is. A phone’s own height is good to 10 to 20 m at best, which is why the terrain models, not the phone, supply the answer.
Limits and credits

HARD DECK compares models with each other. It does not know the truth, and it is not a certified source of aeronautical data. For survey-grade heights use levelled benchmarks, or long-occupation GNSS with a national geoid model. Indian charts are tied to the national vertical datum, which can differ from EGM96 by a few decimetres; that is below the noise of any 30 m model.
